## Decisions: 
The most important decision I made during this project was cutting Three.js from the build. I chose to let it go because I would have spent more time trying to learn the library than making progress on the project. I needed working dogs on a screen, not a half-finished experimentation. The second-most important decision during this project was being upfront about using Claude and FigmaMake to help write the code. This felt important to acknowledge because the physics-based movement and Pomodoro integration were beyond what I could execute alone in the time constraints of one semester as a beginner, and being honest about that was not only policy, but felt better than trying to pass it as my own. Additionally, I decided to name the dogs after my real dogs, Luna and Arlo, in order to make the project feel personal, and it motivated me to adjust behaviors and treat drops to their real-life tendencies.
## What worked: 
Throughout the process of developing this project, the core interaction exceeded my expectations. The use of FigmaMake was beneficial in the early stages of development because it allowed me to ship a product that functioned, even when the code got complex, so I could focus on adding features that were important to me and refining visual and spatial features. I am most satisfied with the responsiveness of the treat collecting and chasing of the cursor from the dogs. The user does not require thorough instructions when it flows correctly.
## What I would do differently: 
If I started over, I would change the order I worked in. I would spend more time in Figma designing what Luna and Arlo actually look like as characters before touching any JavaScript. I would experiment with Three.js rom the get-go to test the feasibility of creating look-alike characters before defaulting to HTML and CSS. I would change my approach to tools by utilizing AI in a way that focuses on logic instead of only the output, so when bugs appear I'm not searching through code I don't fully understand. I would change my scope plan to decide earlier which features were essential and lay out time to finish those rather than treating refinement like something that happens after everything else technically works. The tennis ball cursor size issue, arguably one of the most noticeable design flaws, kept getting pushed to "should complete" on my iteration plan until it was too late.
## What I learned: 
Because of the Luna and Arlo project, I learned that scope is also a design decision and not just a logistics problem. I learned that I care a lot more about finishing something when it feels personal; putting real names on the characters changed how invested I was. With my tools, I learned that AI can get you further than you may get with your own knowledge and skills, but the gap between understanding what the code does versus why it does it will show up the moment something doesn't work. This was apparent in my borders. For the process, I learned that movement and interaction can be solid while the visual design around them falls flat, and that disconnect is something users notice. I also have some ideas on what I would want to build next if I came back to this; Three.js is on the list.
